Why This Falls on RTM Boards

When leaseholders take on the Right to Manage, the RTM company becomes legally responsible for the building — not just day-to-day management, but the harder things too, including fire safety and, where it's needed, external wall remediation. Most RTM directors are volunteers: leaseholders who put themselves forward to get better management of their block, not to become experts in PAS 9980, government funding schemes or construction procurement.

That gap — between what the role legally requires and what any reasonable volunteer director can be expected to know — is exactly where most RTM boards get stuck. Not because the building's problem is unusual, but because the process of dealing with it properly is unfamiliar, slow-moving, and involves several different specialists who don't naturally talk to each other unless someone makes them.

What RTM Directors Are Actually Responsible For

As the entity with legal responsibility for the building, an RTM company is typically the Responsible Entity for fire safety and remediation purposes. In practice, that generally means the board is responsible for making sure:

  • A proper assessment of the external wall fire risk is commissioned where there's reason to think one is needed — see our guide to whether your building needs a FRAEW.
  • Any findings are acted on appropriately, whether that's interim safety measures or full remediation.
  • Government funding routes, such as the Cladding Safety Scheme or Building Safety Fund, are explored where the building may be eligible.
  • Leaseholders and residents are kept properly informed throughout, including where EWS1 forms are needed for sales or remortgages.
  • Records are kept of what's been done, by whom, and on what basis — increasingly important under the Building Safety Act.

None of this requires the board itself to become technically expert. It does require someone to make sure the right specialists are appointed, briefed correctly, and kept moving — which is where a coordinator like Wrightwood typically comes in.

Not legal advice

This page explains the general position in practical terms. It isn't legal advice, and it doesn't tell you what your specific RTM company is required to do for your building. Your company's own legal and professional advisers are best placed to confirm your obligations and any personal exposure for directors.

Common Situations RTM Boards Find Themselves In

Every building is different, but most RTM boards we speak to recognise one of these starting points:

A lender has requested an EWS1A leaseholder's sale or remortgage is stalling because the lender wants an EWS1, and the board isn't sure what's already in place or what needs commissioning. See FRAEW vs EWS1.

A FRAEW has flagged a problemAn assessment has come back identifying risk, and the board now needs to work out what remediation involves, what it costs, and how to fund it. See what happens after a FRAEW.

Residents are raising concernsLeaseholders are asking questions the board can't confidently answer, and the directors want a clear, honest position to communicate back.

No one has looked at this yetThe board suspects there may be an issue — construction type, building height, or general uncertainty — but nothing formal has been commissioned.

Why RTM Boards Choose to Bring In a Coordinator

The directors we work with are usually capable people managing this alongside full-time jobs and their own lives — the issue is rarely ability, it's time, unfamiliarity with the process, and the risk of getting something procedurally wrong on a project that only happens once. Bringing in a coordinator means the board retains oversight and decision-making, while someone else carries the day-to-day technical and administrative load, chases the specialists, and keeps the project moving between board meetings.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions from RTM company directors.

01

Can our RTM company apply for the Cladding Safety Scheme?

Yes, potentially. An RTM company can act as the Responsible Entity where it holds the relevant legal responsibility for the building and its remediation. See our FRAEW & Cladding Safety Scheme FAQ for more detail.

02

Does appointing a coordinator cost the RTM company directly, or can it come from the service charge?

This depends on your lease terms and the basis on which the cost is being incurred, which is a matter for your company's own legal and financial advisers. In our experience many RTM companies fund this type of professional support through the service charge where it falls within the terms of the lease, in the same way as other professional fees.

03

Do all our directors need to be involved in every decision?

That's a matter of your company's own governance arrangements rather than something we can generalise about. In practice, most boards nominate one or two directors as the main point of contact for day-to-day coordination, while bringing significant decisions back to the full board.

04

We don't have any in-house building safety expertise — does that matter?

No — it's the normal starting position for most RTM boards, and exactly why a coordination role exists. Our job is to supply the technical process and specialist appointments the board needs, while the directors retain oversight and decision-making.

05

What's the first thing we should do if we suspect our cladding is unsafe?

Start by gathering whatever building, fire safety and external wall information you already have, and get an initial view from a suitably qualified professional on whether a full FRAEW is warranted. Our guide to whether you need a FRAEW walks through this in more detail, or you can get in touch and we'll help you work out the right first step.
